A chart builder
you can talk to

Role Product Designer, end to end
Company SafetyCulture
Domain Analytics
Platform Web
Status Launch ready (design & spec)
Key result Baseline: ~45% completion rate before redesign

Overview

I designed a conversational, AI-powered chart builder to fix the #1 complaint about SafetyCulture Analytics: that building a chart was too manual and fiddly to bother with.

The problem

Manual, fiddly chart-building → charts created through conversation

Completion lagged badly for anyone who started building a chart, between Nov 2024 and Mar 2025. In customer calls, "the learning curve" came up repeatedly — the existing builder asked users to make a series of configuration decisions before they saw anything useful.

How I got here

The drop-off data pointed at a specific failure: the old builder made users answer every question before showing them anything. So the design inverts it — show a chart first, refine after. Given even just a metric, the system generates a chart with sensible defaults, then asks the single most valuable follow-up question, in a fixed hierarchy: metric → attribute → filters and chart type → configuration. That order wasn't arbitrary — it mirrors the existing chart builder's sequence, so the AI asks questions in the same order customers already built charts manually.

Just as important is what I designed out. Editing inside the chat was cut — it blurred "save" and "edit" and duplicated the dashboard editor. Saving multiple charts per session was cut — mismatched filters break a dashboard's global filters. Click-based chart editing was cut — syncing chart interactions back to the model added complexity V1 didn't need. And the unglamorous half of the work: error and clarification flows, session and token limits, and a model-agnostic UI that survives the underlying model being swapped.

The solution

A conversational chart builder on the Analytics landing page: describe what you want, get a chart instantly, refine it through guided follow-ups. V1 was deliberately limited to that single entry point — dashboard-level creation introduced filter-consistency issues and outstripped design capacity.

Try the interactive prototype

Outcome

Design and spec were complete and the project was entering back-end review — with a staged rollout planned (dogfood → closed EA → GA) — when the roadmap shifted and the release was paused. Success metrics were defined up front: lift completion past the ~45% baseline, and track the share of charts created via natural language as the adoption signal.

Making one chart
do the work of five

Role Product Designer, end to end
Company SafetyCulture
Domain Analytics
Platform Web
Status Released
Key result Demand: $770K AUD in linked ARR

Overview

Customers used KPI thresholds to judge performance at a glance — but charts supported only a single KPI, with no custom ranges or colour coding. I designed multiple-KPI support across SafetyCulture's chart library, letting customers track several targets and performance bands in a single chart.

The problem

Several near-identical charts and spreadsheet exports → one chart tracking every target

Without multiple KPIs or custom thresholds, customers resorted to two workarounds: creating several near-identical charts, or exporting data to spreadsheets and analysing it outside the platform. Both were time-consuming and undermined the value of Analytics itself. Demand was well-evidenced across customer calls and the product feedback board.

How I got here

The demand was already quantified, so the design question wasn't whether — it was how to add complexity without breaking what customers already understood. The platform had one existing KPI behaviour: bar colours change when a single target is met. Rather than invent a new visual language, I extended that mental model to multiple targets, and split configuration into two modes — single-value lines for targets, min/max range bands for performance zones — so users only see the options their job needs.

The design was pressure-tested through rounds of front-end engineering review and internal dogfooding — including a debate over how many KPI lines a chart could reasonably carry, which is where the shipped caps were settled. The hardest constraint was consistency: one configuration pattern that behaves predictably across seven chart types, from stacked columns to dials.

The solution

A KPI configuration modal with two modes: single-value KPIs — up to 4 per chart, shown as line indicators with custom names and colours — and range KPIs — up to 5 performance bands defined by min/max values. The design extended the platform's existing bar-colour behaviour to multiple targets, keeping the mental model customers already had, across seven chart types: stacked, column, grouped, bar, line, timetable, and dial.

Outcome

Released to all customers on web. Post-release impact wasn't instrumented, so the honest answer is: shipped, impact not tracked — a gap I'd push to close earlier if I ran this project again.

A 2013 photo editor,
rebuilt for today

Role Product Designer, end to end
Company SafetyCulture
Domain SafetyCulture App
Platform Mobile
Status Shipped
Key result Access: colourblind-safe palette, widened tap targets

Overview

For frontline workers, annotating a photo is how evidence gets made — circling the hazard, marking the defect. But the mobile editing experience they relied on was built in 2013 and had barely changed since. I redesigned it end to end, validated the new pattern with user testing, and it shipped.

The problem

An editor unchanged since 2013 → editing patterns workers already know

The iOS editor dated back to 2013 and its flows were a long way from what anyone now expects from a photo tool. Every inspection that needed an annotated image ran through that friction. The project goal was to bring the user flow and UI in line with current-day intuitive editing experiences.

How I got here

The guiding decision was not to reinvent the wheel: every worker's phone has already taught them how a photo editor behaves, so the new design borrows those conventions instead of inventing proprietary ones. I prototyped the new screen pattern in Figma and validated it before build with a remote preference test — five participants, managers and frontline workers across construction, manufacturing, and utilities in four countries. Navigation proved intuitive; participants moved between features without prompting. The test also caught a language problem I'd have missed: "Original" and "Done" didn't clearly say what they did once edits auto-saved — a copy fix that went straight into the design.

The design work also fed the build decision: after evaluating third-party editing SDKs with engineering, the editor was rebuilt natively — the first release mirroring existing features under the new UI, on an architecture that let follow-on features (brightness, crop, rotate) ship behind flags roughly a week apart.

Native also meant designing for real device diversity, not one fixed canvas: I tested the toolbar and editing surface across screen sizes from iPhone SE to iPhone 16 and Android Compact, letting layout and aspect ratio adapt per platform (3:4 on iOS, 2:3 on Android) instead of stretching a single design across all of them. System gestures stayed untouched — edge-swipe-back on iOS, the Android back button — and pinch-to-zoom worked exactly as people already expected from their camera roll.

Building both platforms natively from scratch also meant accessibility couldn't be inherited for free — there was no default OS component quietly doing that work for us, so every decision had to be made on purpose. Toolbar icons carry visible text labels rather than relying on icon-only affordances, and tap targets were widened directly in the spec: icon padding went from 16px up to 20px on one row and 24px on the other, specifically to give people a bigger hit area than the icon's visual size implied. The annotation colour palette was chosen with colourblind accessibility in mind too, so tool and state differences don't rely on colour alone.

The solution

A rebuilt mobile editing experience: pen and shape annotation, arrows, text with styling, colour and stroke controls, rotate and flip, undo/redo — in a carousel-based UI I designed natively for both iOS and Android.

Outcome

Shipped to customers through a staged rollout, with the redesigned pattern validated by user testing before a line of code was written.

Which problem
do we solve first?

Role Product Designer
Company SafetyCulture
Domain Analytics
Platform Web
Status Discovery — cut short

Overview

When Analytics work was set to resume, the domain team held three competing beliefs about what was most broken — and no evidence-based way to choose between them. I designed a two-phase discovery project to make that call — not to design a solution, but to define the problem sharply enough for the team to commit — and ran its first phase.

The problem

Three competing problem hypotheses → one evidence-backed priority

The three hypotheses: a legacy experience gap (the top feedback on Analytics was that users missed the old experience), a chart builder with no scalable version, and low discoverability of Analytics features. The brief was explicitly not a solution — it was a sharp, evidenced problem definition with a recommended priority order the team could act on.

How I got here

I structured the work as the Discover and Define halves of the Double Diamond, run deliberately data-first: before recruiting a single interviewee, Phase 1 mined every source the company already had — Amplitude and Hex behavioural funnels, Gong call reviews, a 90-day Intercom pull plus the 2024 migration period, ProductBoard requests, Salesforce win/loss and churn signals, and competitive benchmarks. Each source had a defined job: behavioural data surfaces symptoms like drop-offs and abandoned sessions, calls and support tickets explain cause, Salesforce shows business impact. The themes from that phase defined mutually exclusive customer cohorts, so Phase 2 interviews validated with exactly the right people instead of a convenience sample.

The research questions were segmented by usage: power users were asked what almost made them give up and why they didn't; casual users what keeps them coming back and what stops them going deeper; low-usage users what it's like just working out what to do. And because a priority call is only useful if people trust it, I applied an explicit decision-making framework — weighing institutional knowledge, then user-familiarity signals, then behavioural data at scale — with the rationale documented, and the synthesis peer-reviewed by engineering, product, and design before stakeholder sign-off.

The solution

The deliverable was designed as a prioritised, evidence-backed problem definition: an insight statement per problem area (observation, evidence, implication), a structured problem statement (context, user, pain, impact), defined customer cohorts ready for targeted recruitment, and a recommended priority order with the reasoning shown — so the team could disagree with the conclusion without distrusting the process.

Outcome

The project ended where Phase 1 did: my role was made redundant before synthesis was complete — no priority call was made, and the validation interviews were never run. What remains is the framework: the research plan, the decision-making rationale, the interview guides, and the Phase 1 evidence base were all documented to a standard where whoever picks the problem up next doesn't start from zero.

Next steps

Phase 2 was scoped to validate the Phase 1 hypotheses with the customer cohorts it defined — moderated interviews against a Lean UX Canvas per cohort, plus further Salesforce and Hex signal analysis. None of it ran; the board above is exactly where the project stopped.
