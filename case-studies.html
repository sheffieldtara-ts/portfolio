A chart builder
you can talk to

Role Product Designer, end to end
Company SafetyCulture
Domain Analytics
Platform Web
Status In backend build — paused for AI special projects
Timeframe March – May 2025

Overview

This started as a mandate from product leadership to find AI's shape within each domain — not a specific feature brief. We matched it to the #1 complaint about SafetyCulture Analytics: building a chart was too manual, and the mental load was high enough that people couldn't tell if finishing was worth it, so they dropped off partway through. I designed a conversational, AI-powered chart builder to fix it.

The problem

Manual, fiddly chart-building → charts created through conversation

Before: the existing builder (pictured) made users pick a metric, then an attribute, then filters, then a chart type — in that order, with nothing shown until every step was complete. That surfaced in three places: a live dashboard tracking completion between Nov 2024 and Mar 2025 (the baseline above), a cluster of customer calls specifically flagged for "learning curve" and "too complex" language, and matching demand on the product feedback board. We treated it as a testable hypothesis — that natural language input would lower the barrier to entry and lift completion — rather than a hunch.

How I got here

There wasn't time to run new customer calls due to an impending AI project we were all about to be placed on, so instead, I analysed the prompts and feedback coming through SafetyCulture's existing AI Assistant (Closed EA at the time). A pattern jumped out: people kept asking what their data actually meant, then asking to see it as a graph — something Assistant couldn't do. We also had collated ProductBoard data where there was a high indication that "chart builder experience is difficult to use" was a top pain point for customers. That, plus the drop-off data, pointed at a specific failure: the current builder made users answer every question before showing them anything.

The resulting insight signalled the design needed to invert the current experience — show a chart first, refine after. Given even just a metric, the system generates a chart with sensible defaults, then asks the single most valuable follow-up question, in a fixed hierarchy: metric → attribute → filters and chart type → configuration. That order wasn't arbitrary — it mirrors the existing chart builder's sequence, so the AI asks questions in the same order customers already built charts manually.

Mapping the full flow space took real focus: every branch had to account for both the existing chart builder's mechanics and how early the underlying AI model still was — would people know what to ask, would they trust a chart they didn't build themselves. I also used the project to establish the first UI patterns and components for AI interactions in the product, working closely with our design systems designer so the rest of the design team could reuse them for the AI features that came after.

The solution

A conversational chart builder on the Analytics landing page: describe what you want, get a chart instantly, refine it through guided follow-ups.

V1 was to achieve:

  • a single entry point on the landing page, to ensure no overlap with other AI features being introduced
  • suggested prompts of frequently sought-after data combinations, for ease of use
  • AI guidance to steer chart creation through follow-up responses
  • clear guidance for saving to an existing or new dashboard
  • clear next steps for users who hit an error message
Try the interactive prototype

Outcome

Design and spec were complete and the project was entering back-end review — with a staged rollout planned (dogfood → closed EA → GA) — when the roadmap shifted and the release was paused, before it reached that first dogfood stage. No usability testing ran as a result. Success metrics were defined up front for when it did: lift completion past the ~45% baseline, and track the share of charts created via natural language as the adoption signal.

Making one chart
do the work of five

Role Product Designer, end to end
Company SafetyCulture
Domain Analytics
Platform Web
Status Released
Timeframe May – July 2025

Overview

Customers used KPI thresholds to judge performance at a glance — but charts only supported a single pass/fail-style target, with no way to benchmark or bucket their data into their own bands (poor, fair, good, great — or however their business needed to read it). Giving customers that flexibility was a priority for the Analytics team, so I designed multiple-KPI support across SafetyCulture's chart library, letting customers track several targets and performance bands in a single chart.

The problem

Several near-identical charts and spreadsheet exports → one chart tracking every target

Without multiple KPIs or custom thresholds, customers resorted to two workarounds: creating several near-identical charts, or exporting data to spreadsheets and analysing it outside the platform. Both were time-consuming and undermined the value of Analytics itself. Demand was well-evidenced across customer calls and the product feedback board.

How I got here

The demand was already quantified, so the design question wasn't whether — it was how to add complexity without breaking what customers already understood. The platform had one existing KPI behaviour: bar colours change when a single target is met. Rather than invent a new visual language, I extended that mental model to multiple targets, and split configuration into two modes — single-value lines for targets, min/max range bands for performance zones — so users only see the options their job needs.

The design was pressure-tested through rounds of front-end engineering review and internal dogfooding — including a debate over how many KPI lines a chart could reasonably carry, which is where the shipped caps were settled. The hardest constraint was consistency: one configuration pattern that behaves predictably across seven chart types, from stacked columns to dials.

The solution

A KPI configuration modal with two modes: single-value KPIs — up to 4 per chart, shown as line indicators with custom names and colours — and range KPIs — up to 5 performance bands defined by min/max values. The design extended the platform's existing bar-colour behaviour to multiple targets, keeping the mental model customers already had, across seven chart types: stacked, column, grouped, bar, line, timetable, and dial.

Outcome

Released to all customers on web. Formal usage metrics weren't instrumented, but customer conversations after release surfaced one thing clearly: colour customisation was the standout. Customers could finally set their own KPI colours — either as a traffic-light system, or matched to their brand — so charts dropped into reports looking consistent, something they'd never been able to do with bars or columns before. It was one of the most-requested asks we heard.

A 2013 photo editor,
rebuilt for today

Role Product Designer, end to end
Company SafetyCulture
Domain SafetyCulture App
Platform Mobile
Status Shipped
Timeframe Aug – Oct 2024

Overview

For frontline workers, annotating a photo is how evidence gets made — circling the hazard, marking the defect. But the mobile editing experience they relied on was built in 2013 and had barely changed since. I redesigned it end to end, validated the new pattern with user testing, and it shipped.

The problem

An editor unchanged since 2013 → editing patterns workers already know

The old editor (pictured above) dated back to 2013 and its flows were a long way from what anyone now expects from a photo tool. Every inspection that needed an annotated image ran through that friction. The project goal was to bring the user flow and UI in line with current-day intuitive editing experiences.

How I got here

It had been a known problem for a long time without anyone owning it — a Principal PM pushed to finally get it prioritized, and I put my hand up to lead it.

The guiding decision was not to reinvent the wheel: every worker's phone had already taught them how a photo editor behaves, so the redesign borrows those conventions instead of inventing proprietary ones.

Validated before build with a remote preference test — 5 participants (managers and frontline workers) across construction, manufacturing, and utilities, in 4 countries:

  • Navigation proved intuitive — participants moved between features without prompting.
  • One participant, who takes frequent photos for compliance reporting, called out how much the simplicity would help in her day-to-day.
  • Caught a language problem I'd have missed: a participant pointed out that if edits auto-save, "Done" doesn't actually mean anything — the labels didn't match what was happening. Fixed before build.

Build decision: worked closely with a knowledgeable Principal PM throughout, and with the iOS and Android engineers building both front and back end — a genuinely great collaboration.

  • Evaluated third-party editing SDKs with engineering together, then chose to rebuild natively.
  • First release mirrored existing features under the new UI; the architecture let follow-on features (brightness, crop, rotate) ship behind flags about a week apart.

Device diversity: the part I enjoyed most — researching how individual editing features are natively used and expected to behave on each platform, then designing toward something that would feel intuitive on both, not just familiar on one.

  • Tested the toolbar and editing surface from iPhone SE to iPhone 16, and Android Compact.
  • Layout and aspect ratio adapt per platform (3:4 iOS, 2:3 Android) instead of stretching one design across all of them.
  • System gestures stayed untouched (edge-swipe-back on iOS, the Android back button); pinch-to-zoom works exactly as people expect from their camera roll.

Technical limitation: accessibility wasn't free. Building both platforms natively meant there was no default OS component quietly handling it — every decision had to be made on purpose:

  • Toolbar icons carry visible text labels, not icon-only affordances.
  • Tap targets widened directly in the spec — icon padding went from 16px to 20px on one row, 24px on the other, for a bigger hit area than the icon's visual size implied.
  • Annotation colour palette chosen with colourblind accessibility in mind, so tool and state differences don't rely on colour alone.

The solution

A rebuilt mobile editing experience: pen and shape annotation, arrows, text with styling, colour and stroke controls, rotate and flip, undo/redo — in a carousel-based UI I designed natively for both iOS and Android.

Outcome

Shipped to customers through a staged rollout, with the redesigned pattern validated by user testing before a line of code was written.

Which problem
do we solve first?

Role Product Designer
Company SafetyCulture
Domain Analytics
Platform Web
Status Discovery — cut short
Timeframe May – June 2026

Overview

When Analytics work was set to resume, the domain team held three competing beliefs about what was most broken — and no evidence-based way to choose between them. I designed a two-phase discovery project to make that call — not to design a solution, but to define the problem sharply enough for the team to commit — and ran its first phase.

The problem

Three competing problem hypotheses → one evidence-backed priority

The three hypotheses:

  • a legacy experience gap — the top feedback on Analytics was that users missed the old experience
  • a chart builder with no scalable version
  • low discoverability of Analytics features

The brief was explicitly not a solution — it was a sharp, evidenced problem definition with a recommended priority order the team could act on.

Define

Research questions

The research questions were segmented by usage:

  • power users: asked what almost made them give up and why they didn't
  • casual users: what keeps them coming back and what stops them going deeper
  • low-usage users: what it's like just working out what to do
Prioritisation

And because a priority call is only useful if people trust it, I applied an explicit decision-making framework — weighing institutional knowledge, then user-familiarity signals, then behavioural data at scale — with the rationale documented, and the synthesis peer-reviewed by engineering, product, and design before stakeholder sign-off.

How I got here

I structure my research using the Double Diamond method, starting at Discover and Define.

Discover

Data

Phase 1 mined every data source the company already had:

  • Amplitude and Hex behavioural funnels
  • "Gong" customer call reviews
  • a 90-day Intercom pull, as well as the 2024 migration period
  • ProductBoard data — aggregated customer feedback
  • Salesforce win/loss and churn signals (phase 2)
  • competitive benchmarks (phase 2)
Synthesis

Each source had a defined job:

  • behavioural data surfaces symptoms like drop-offs and abandoned sessions
  • calls and support tickets explain cause
  • Salesforce shows business impact
Hypotheses

The themes from phase 1 defined mutually exclusive customer cohorts, so Phase 2 interviews validated with exactly the right people instead of a convenience sample.

The solution

The deliverable was designed as a prioritised, evidence-backed problem definition: an insight statement per problem area (observation, evidence, implication), a structured problem statement (context, user, pain, impact), defined customer cohorts ready for targeted recruitment, and a recommended priority order with the reasoning shown — so the team could disagree with the conclusion without distrusting the process.

Outcome

The project ended where Phase 1 did: my role was made redundant before synthesis was complete — no priority call was made, and the validation interviews were never run. What remains is the framework: the research plan, the decision-making rationale, the interview guides, and the Phase 1 evidence base were all documented to a standard where whoever picks the problem up next doesn't start from zero.

Next steps

Phase 2 was scoped to validate the Phase 1 hypotheses with the customer cohorts it defined — moderated interviews against a Lean UX Canvas per cohort, plus further Salesforce and Hex signal analysis. None of it ran; the board above is exactly where the project stopped.
